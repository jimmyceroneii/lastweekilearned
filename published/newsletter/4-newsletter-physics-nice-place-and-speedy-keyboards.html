Physics, Nice Places, and Speedy Keyboards | Newsletter #4

Jimmy Cerone

08 Jan 2022 — 3 min read
Share

I read lots of articles these days (and other content) and as part of my morning pages, I write about them. In the spirit of selling my by-products, I’m compiling my favorite ten things from the week to further pollute your backlog.

  1. Reality Is Not What It Seems by Carlos Rovelli — Absolutely earth shaking shallow dive into physics.
Funny that back in the day, I considered a whole book a shallow dive. Oh the joys of being 23 without any real responsibilities... What's funny is I do not remember any of this book at all. While I love audio books, this is a strike against them for academic reading. 
  1. Privacy is a collective concern — I imagined this article would be about Google and Facebook but it took a scary turn and talked about the privacy of our genes instead…
I am scared by the predictions that came true in this article. Roughly 4 years after publication, 23andMe suffered a data breach. 6 years after publication, all of the data almost fell into unfamiliar hands after 23andMe filed for chapter 11 bankruptcy, saved by the founder Anne Wojcicki outspending Regeneron for it. 
  1. This Keyboard Lets People Type So Fast It’s Banned From Typing Competitions — The strangest typing device I’ve ever seen. And I own this strange typing device.
With the rise of dictation thanks to AI, I wonder how these strange devices are holding up. While I'm still using my strange typing device, I'm uncertain how many others will be willing to eat the learning curve when you can talk to your computer instead. 
  1. Our Self Imposed Scarcity of Nice Places — A strange mix of depressing and hopeful article about the future of housing.
Living in Pittsburgh the last couple of years gave me new perspective on this article. Pittsburgh is one of the last places in America where you affordable, lovely urban living exists. Now that is not to say Pittsburgh is perfect and the distribution of that living is equitable, but it is a data point supporting this article. Nice places need not be scarce. 
  1. The Cold Start Problem by Andrew Chen — The first book I’ve seen to attempt to define and pin down network effects. Well worth a read.
While this book was probably useful, I can't say I remember much of it now. These days my thinking is less devoted to scaling gigantic companies and more to growing healthy cities. Surprisingly different problems. 
  1. The Troublesome “Active Record” Pattern — A hyper nerd deep dive into a problematic database structure from the author of one of my all time favorite books Deep Work.
I do not remember the particulars of this article, but it did start me down the path of reading hard technical papers. I don't do it as often as I'd like, but even random shallow dives into quality papers in your field can be surprisingly useful.
  1. The Brilliant Life and Tragic Death of Aaron Swartz — A fascinating article about a riveting programmer that touches on everything from open source to censorship to conspiracy.
As I reflect on this article, now older myself than Shwartz was at the time of his death, I feel a deep sense of melancholy. A life cut short is always tragic, but I sense we need people of great conscience in tech now more than ever. 
  1. Teamflow — My favorite virtual office software to date. Like your real office except in Minecraft.
Ah the days of the COVID virtual office. Wild how quickly the visions of the metaverse faded away. 
  1. Karst Stone Paper — These notebooks are unbelievable and you just have to try one.
Echoing this. I buy one a year as a treat to myself. Their puzzles make an excellent gift!
  1. beautiful trouble — An album from an artist who is quickly becoming my favorite: Nahko And Medicine for The People
